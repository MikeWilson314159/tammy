2 · THE BIG IDEA

Why do we have algebra?

Arithmetic finds answers using known numbers. Algebra lets us reason about numbers that are unknown or changing.

Arithmetic
3 + 5 = 8

We know both numbers.

Algebra
x + 5 = 8

We don't know x yet. What number makes the statement true?

Ask: What does the equal sign mean? How could you check that x = 3?

The equal sign means both sides have the same value. Substituting 3 gives 3 + 5 = 8, which is true.
3 · ONE UNKNOWN

Expressions, equations, and solving

A variable is a symbol representing a number. An expression has no equal sign; an equation says two expressions have equal values.

Expression
3x + 5

We can evaluate it if we know x.

Equation
3x + 5 = 20

We can solve for the unknown.

Work one step at a time

3x + 5 = 20
3x = 15 (subtract 5 from both sides)
x = 5 (divide both sides by 3)

Check: 3(5) + 5 = 20. A solution is a value that makes the original equation true.

4 · TWO UNKNOWNS

What if there are two variables?

Consider x + y = 8. There are many pairs that make this true. Enter a value for x and watch y change.

x = 3, y = 5

Some solutions: (0, 8), (1, 7), (3, 5), and (8, 0). One equation with two unknowns does not determine a unique pair.

Now add a second condition

x + y = 8
x − y = 2

Which pair makes both equations true?

(5, 3) because 5 + 3 = 8 and 5 − 3 = 2. The second equation narrows the possible pairs down to one.

Teaching note: As a general rule, a unique solution to two unknowns requires two independent conditions. Two equations alone do not guarantee one solution.

5 · HANDS-ON

Linear equations make straight lines

Place one uncooked piece of spaghetti on graph paper. It models part of a straight line. Rotate it, move it, and ask: is it still straight? A linear equation in two variables describes a line.

Now place a second piece of spaghetti on the paper. Explore three possibilities:

Cross once

One common point → one solution.

Never cross

Parallel distinct lines → no solution.

Overlap

Same line → infinitely many solutions.

Ask: Can two different straight lines cross more than once? What does crossing mean if each line represents an equation?

6 · CONNECT THE IDEAS

What is a system of linear equations?

A system is a set of equations considered together. A solution must satisfy every equation in the system.

y = x + 1
y = −x + 5

Each equation produces a line. Their intersection is (2, 3), and it satisfies both equations:

3 = 2 + 1 ✓
3 = −2 + 5 ✓

Try changing the equations in the calculator and predict whether the lines will cross, stay parallel, or overlap.

7 · SOLVE ALGEBRAICALLY

Three ways to solve a system

Graphing

Draw both lines and find their intersection. Useful for seeing the meaning of the answer.

Substitution

Replace a variable using an equivalent expression. Useful when one variable is already isolated.

Elimination

Add or subtract equations to eliminate a variable. Useful when coefficients line up.

Example: both equations equal y

y = 2x + 1
y = −x + 7

Because both right-hand expressions equal y, set them equal to each other:

2x + 1 = −x + 7
3x + 1 = 7
3x = 6
x = 2
y = 2(2) + 1 = 5

Solution: (2, 5). Verify in the second equation: 5 = −2 + 7 ✓

8 · THE BIG IDEA

Why elimination works

Goal: turn two equations with two unknowns into an equation with only one unknown. We do not add equations just to get another equation; we add them strategically to make a variable disappear.

Discovery 1 · Adding true equations gives another true equation

x = 3
y = 5
────────
x + y = 8

The point (3, 5) satisfies the new equation. More generally, any point that satisfies both original equations also satisfies their sum. But the new equation by itself may contain other points.

Think first: If two lines meet at a point, will the equation formed by adding their equations pass through that point?
Yes. At that point, both equalities are true. Adding their left sides and their right sides preserves equality.

Discovery 2 · Make a variable disappear

x + y = 10
x − y = −8
──────────
2x + 0y = 2
2x = 2 → x = 1

The opposite y terms cancel: +y − y = 0. Substitute x = 1 into either original equation to find y = 9. The solution is (1, 9). On a graph, the new equation x = 1 is a vertical line through the original intersection.

Discovery 3 · Adding without a plan may not help

2x + 3y = 1
x + y = 9
──────────
3x + 4y = 10

This sum is valid, but both variables remain. We want opposite coefficients, so that one pair adds to zero.

Discovery 4 · Scaling changes the equation's appearance, not its line

x + y = 2
Multiply EVERY term by 2:
2x + 2y = 4

These are the same line. Every point satisfying one also satisfies the other. Multiplying an entire equation by a nonzero number preserves its solution set. We can multiply one equation or both to create opposite coefficients.

Important: Never multiply only one term. Multiplying by zero is not reversible and does not preserve the same line. When subtracting equations, subtract the entire left side and the entire right side, including signs.

Worked example · Scale one equation

2x + 3y = 1
x + y = 9

Multiply the second equation by −3:
−3x − 3y = −27

Add:
(2x + 3y) + (−3x − 3y) = 1 + (−27)
−x = −26 → x = 26

Substitute into x + y = 9:
26 + y = 9 → y = −17

Check: 2(26) + 3(−17) = 1 and 26 + (−17) = 9. Solution: (26, −17).

Worked example · Scale both equations

2x + 3y = 12
3x − 2y = 13

Multiply the first by 2:
4x + 6y = 24
Multiply the second by 3:
9x − 6y = 39

Add:
13x = 63 → x = 63/13
Then 2(63/13) + 3y = 12
3y = 30/13 → y = 10/13

Both equations were scaled because the y coefficients, 3 and −2, did not yet have equal magnitudes. Teaching question: Could we instead eliminate x? Yes—multiply the first equation by 3 and the second by −2.

Interactive elimination lab

Choose multipliers for the two equations. Watch what happens to their coefficients. Try to make either the x coefficients or the y coefficients add to zero.

2x + 3y = 1
x + y = 9

Student challenge: Why is multiplying the second equation by −3 useful? Could you use positive 3 and subtract instead?
Multiplying by −3 creates −3y, which cancels +3y when added. Multiplying by +3 also works if you subtract the scaled second equation from the first.

When should I scale one equation or both?

If coefficients already match or are opposites, add or subtract immediately. If one coefficient is a convenient multiple of the other, scale just one equation. Otherwise, choose a common multiple and scale both. Always check the answer in the original system.
